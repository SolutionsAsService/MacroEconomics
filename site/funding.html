Funding public goods

Many small gifts beat one large one: try it.

In plain words: quadratic funding is a way to share out a pot of matching money. Each project gets more of the pot the more people back it, not the more money they give. Change the numbers below and watch the pot move.

Three projects and a matching pool

Each number is one person's contribution. Example: "10, 10, 10" is three people giving $10 each.

What each project receives

ProjectContributionsTotal givenBefore scaling: (Σ√c)² − ΣcMatchReceives
Project A1 × $100$100$0$0$100
Project B10 × $10$100$900$750$850
Project C4 × $25$100$300$250$350

With the starting numbers, Project A's one $100 gift earns no match at all, while Project B's ten $10 gifts, the same $100 in total, take $750 of the $1,000 pool.

The rule

For each project, add up the square roots of its contributions, square that, and subtract what was given: (Σ√c)² − Σc. Then scale every project's result by the same factor so that all the matches add up to the pool. A project receives its contributions plus its match. With one contributor the result is zero; with many small ones it grows fast.

The inherited data stated it as "Direct contributions + (Square of the sum of the square roots of individual contributions) * Matching pool", which multiplies by the whole pool and would pay out many times the pot.

The rule as set out by Vitalik Buterin, Zoë Hitzig and E. Glen Weyl, "A Flexible Design for Funding Public Goods" (2019). Used by Gitcoin Grants and clr.fund.

What it is good at, and where it breaks

For

  • Promotes democratic engagement by valuing the number of contributors more than the size of contributions.
  • Encourages wide participation in the funding process, making it more inclusive.
  • Optimizes for community preference, directing more funds to projects that have broader support.
  • Reduces the influence of large donors, making funding outcomes more equitable.

Against

  • Complexity of the concept can be a barrier to understanding and participation.
  • Requires a significant matching pool to be effective, which depends on the generosity of sponsors.
  • Potential for gaming the system by splitting large donations into smaller ones to maximize matching funds.
  • Implementation and administration can be resource-intensive.

Other ways projects get funded

Venture Capital

Funding provided by investors to startups and small businesses with long-term growth potential.

For: Accelerate growth.; Bring innovations to market.

Examples: Series A, B, C funding rounds.; Angel investing.

Crowdfunding

Raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, typically via the Internet.

For: Validate product-market fit.; Engage with the community.

Examples: Kickstarter campaigns.; GoFundMe for personal or social causes.

Grants

Non-repayable funds or products disbursed by one party, often a government department, corporation, foundation, or trust.

For: Support specific projects or research.; Promote initiatives benefiting the public.

Examples: Research grants from governmental agencies like the NSF or NIH.; Community development grants from foundations.

Debt Financing

Raising funds through borrowing that must be repaid over time, with interest.

For: Fund operations or expansion without diluting ownership.

Examples: Bank loans.; Bonds.

Bootstrapping

Starting and growing a business using personal finance or the operating revenues of the new company.

For: Maintain full control and ownership.; Minimize debt.

Examples: Self-funding.; Reinvesting initial sales back into the business.

Equity Crowdfunding

A form of crowdfunding that gives investors a stake in the company in exchange for capital.

For: Raise capital without traditional venture capital.

Examples: Platforms like SeedInvest or WeFunder.

Sponsorships

Financial support received from an individual or organization in exchange for advertising rights or brand promotion.

For: Fund events, projects, or operations.; Increase brand visibility.

Examples: Corporate sponsorships for events.; Athlete endorsements.

Government Subsidies

Financial assistance provided by the government to support businesses, industries, or projects that are in the public interest.

For: Stimulate economic activity.; Promote social good or innovation.

Examples: Agricultural subsidies.; Renewable energy incentives.

Donation Based Funding

Receiving funds without the expectation of direct financial return, often for non-profits or charitable causes.

For: Support operations and mission-driven activities.

Examples: Online donation platforms like Donorbox.; Charitable giving and endowments.
